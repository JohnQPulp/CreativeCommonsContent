To explain the above:

The `images` array is made up of sub-arrays, each of length 4.

The first item in the subarrays are the names of the various character sprite bases, arranged in alphabetical order based on character identifier name and then grouped by (a)ge modifier. (The modifier will be explained further down.)

The second item in the subarrays is the array of reference images to be used for the image generation. By default, this is set to just be `crefs`, an array with a set of reference sprites for the desired "literary cross-hatching" style, defined like so: `crefs = ["helpers/c-example-1", "helpers/c-example-2", "helpers/c-example-3"]`. However, `crefs` can be replaced with a specific character reference sprite (or, in rare cases, multiple character reference sprites) in the case of doing a sprite update. This ties into:

The third subarray item is the first half of the image generation prompt — the boilerplate half. This has two options: `cprompt` for generating the first variation of a particular character, and `cupdate` for generating a variation of a character based on one previously generated. `crefs` should be used with `cprompt`, whereas a specific character reference like `["c-henry"]` should be used with `cupdate`. The prompt boilerplate starters are defined like:



cprompt = f"""I need you to generate a character sprite for a book character. The style of the character sprite I want generated is a sort of literary illustration style, which I've included a couple examples of for helping guide the output image. The output should follow the style of those examples, but use period-accurate and character-accurate imagery/clothing/etc based on the provided setting and character description and so forth. The image shouldn't have any background art — the background should be of plain paper-like coloring as in the example — and the character shouldn't be holding anything unless specified. The character (which should also have the same plain coloring) should be looking at the viewer, have body posture towards the viewer, and have a neutral expression unless otherwise specified. The illustration should make strong use of hatching/cross-hatching in its style and shading. The illustration should be sized like the example sprites, with similar sized head, cutting off at the legs at the bottom of the image.

This particular book is {bookstring}

The particular character is """

cupdate = f"""I need you to take this character sprite and make and update to it, while keeping the character the same person, and also the art style (literary hatching drawing w/ paper-colored empty background) the same, and also maintaining period-/setting-accuracy for the character. That leaves the fourth argument left — the most important one — the second half of the image generation prompt, which informs the specific details about the character for either initial generation or variation updating. This is the primary task you'll be working towards, being provided the full text of the book and needing to put together accurate character prompts that as correctly as possible cover the details of a character, across their various outfits and ages and other modifiers throughout a book text. Here are the rules and heuristics you should follow as part of this process:

- Each character will have one or more sprites that need generating. One of these needs to serve as the root sprite — the one to be generated using `cprompt` — from which all the others will be generated via `cupdate` prompts off of it, either directly or indirectly.
- Sprites are generated from head-to-knee, roughly, with the heads of the characters at the tops of the image and their legs cut off at the bottom of the images, varying somewhat based on how tall the character is. But in general, it can be assumed that at least part of the sprites' legs will be visible, while their feet generally never will be. Additionally, most updates to the sprites (that aren't large age changes) shouldn't change the sprites' positions — for example their heads should stay in the same places. As a consequence of these two facts, some consideration needs to be given to the *ordering* of how sprites are generated, to ensure that all their variations will fit. Consider for example if a sprite has both hatless and hat-wearing variations. If their hat is short and clings tightly to their head — like for a bowler or cloche — it doesn't necessarily matter what order the sprites are generated in, since the hat should always fit. (In those cases, it's generally better to do the hatless variation first, since it means the root sprite can focus on getting the full hair right.) However, for other hat situations like top hats or tall Edwardian headware, the hat definitely won't fit within the image bounds if generated as part of an update. In those cases, the hatted variations of a sprite need to come first; and if there are multiple different hatted variations (different hat types), the tallest should come first.
- Prompts need to be detailed, incorporating both the explicit and implied details about what a character should look like. Including sort direct quotes from the book as part of prompting is good, and in general care should be spent in analyzing all the information that can be mined about a character's appearance from throughout the full text: clothing, age, height, build, visage, etc. In some cases, this will be enough detail on its own to get a complete distinct character. Other times though, character details will be light — sometimes *too* light. For example, a character's hair style will often not be specified, but leaving a hair style out of the prompt tends to result in characters generated with anachronistic modern-looking hair. Therefore, especially for hair in particular, it's good to specify specific styles, ensuring they make sense in the context of the period.
- Conversely, prompts need to not be *too* detailed. Again, some characters may have enormous descriptions heaped upon them, not all of which should necessarily be included in the prompt, because the more details specified, the less likely the image generator is to get all of them correctly — current image generator technology being good but not perfect. Prompts should focus primarily on the most important bit, especially around the details from the shoulders up, since sprites are usually seen in the corner as just their heads and shoulders. The further down the body, the less important details become to all get exactly correct, until reaching the feet, where shoe specificity is never required (and in fact, is bad, since it confuses the image generator into thinking it needs to do a full-body sprite, thus screwing up the image scaling). It's not often that details should be left out, so don't overindex on this advice, but do take it into account. And later on, it'll be explained how to include skipped details in python comments so that the human reviewer can check to see what was intentionally skipped.
- Prompts need to take into account the final hatching style, which will always be a tan-and-black line-drawing type illustration, reminiscent of the types of drawings found in physical books. One thing this means is that the book's descriptions of specific colors should often be dropped from the prompt. Later color palette normalization will collapse off-style colors anyway, though the normalization works less well the further off from the intended paper-coloring the base generated image starts as. In many cases, there's no need to specify colors at all, as the image generator will be able to safely assume them based on what's being described. In other cases, coloring does need to be specified, but it should generally be done so in terms of hatching density or shading: like for example light vs medium-light vs medium vs medium-dark vs dark/dense hatching. These terms should generally be what's used for describing, for example, hair. Whereas eye color can almost always just be left out of the prompts, since the difference isn't noticeable in the hatching style.
- Prompts should try to make characters visually distinct, within the constraints of still being accurate to both the explicit and implied details about their characters. It's bad to have all characters share the exact same hair style or hair color or exact same clothing style — especially a problem with men's suit clothing. The solution here isn't to anachronistically have the men not wear suits, for example, but to at least try to diversify the suit descriptions within the bounds of what etiquette and historical variations would permit. (Though again, whenever injecting *new* details into the prompts not gotten from the text itself, you'll want to python comment them — again explained further on.) Make liberal use of fancy/obscure hair and clothing terminology and different hat types to help impress variety upon the image generator, who in the absence of specifics, will tend to make characters of the same era all look quite similar. (This is less of an issue across character of different social classes, occupations, etc; so you'll need to be the judge of when extra details are warranted.)
- Prompts should generally not specify emotions onto the character beyond the boilerplate prompt's default neutral emotional state. After the root character sprites is generated and the base variation sprites generated off of it — all a part of this overall subtask you're doing now — expression variation sprites off of those bases will happen in a later task. So in general, a neutral surface is exactly what the base sprites should all start as. Some exceptions are permissible for characters with especially non-neutral dispositions: the overly cheerful or overly snarky or overly downtrodden. Similarly, details about character pose should generally be left out, unless there's an unusual aspect of their posing that should be reflected in the base sprites: such as being hunched or tending to do something particular with their hands or whatever.
- Prompts need to strike a balance between absolute accuracy with the need to not have too many separate tiny variations. For example, there are many implicit details around clothing implied from era etiquette, such as when to wear, for example, hats and gloves. Hats are very important to get right, because the characters' heads are the prominent things, and so special care is put into making sure the characters that need `-xhat` variations sprite variations have them. (So strong is this convention that even characters with only one sprite variation will still carry the `-xhat` marker to emphasize their need for that one sprite to have a hat on.) Conversely, gloves, while having similar detailed social rules around them, aren't as strictly important to get always correct, and so sometimes, even when a character should strictly speaking have both gloves and ungloved variations, only one will be requested, and it becomes the job of the visual editor later on to just make sure hands are only visible when correct (which is usually relatively easy to make happen). Another case of this etiquette compromising is in, say, morning vs evening dresses, which strictly speaking should be separate, but since they only affect the character from the shoulders down, and since the difference is quite subtle in the hatching format, a compromise halfway dress is sometimes just used. (Which simplifies things, especially when the text doesn't make obvious what time of day particular scenes even are.) Keep all this in mind when applying implicit etiquette details: be as accurate as possible given the constrained set of sprites variations chosen from the previous task, even with the knowledge that there might be some technical inaccuracies. If there are, they can be noted in a python comment.
- Prompts doing updates should take care that the order they make the updates in doesn't create too long of update chains. Each update made to a sprite tends to cause a slight degradation to the generated image's hatching style consistency and quality. It's usually unnoticeable after single update, or even after an update of an update, but by the time you get to three or more chained updates, the drop in quality can become distracting. Therefore, after the root sprite is chosen, it's ideal if every variation sprite is made directly off of it. Now, sometimes this isn't possible, like for example if a character needs first a sprite variation that ages them up (e.g., an `-aold` modified), and then another that gives them a hat and overcoat in their older form; then a chain of at least two is necessarily required.
- For the sprite modifiers, note that the (a)ge and e(x)tra naming conventions are rather loose. "Age" doesn't necessarily mean age, but rather any long-lasting change that persists between chapters, and so for example in say, *The Secret Garden*, when Mary goes from sick to well, the transition is captured in `-asick` to `-awell` modifiers, despite her not really aging. At the same time, the "extras" often mean more than just their implied names, like for example `-xhat` might mean adding not just a hat, but also perhaps gloves and an overcoat, or other additional items, just depending upon all of the considerations from above. And then there's the fact that sprites can have multiple different modifier variations like for example `-xhat` vs `-xfur-xhat`, where the shared `hat` name doesn't necessarily mean that both sprites should have the same hat type. The "vibe" hinting further below should help clarify some of the modifier meanings, which you can combine with your own best judgment.



Taking into account all of the above, you can see why some character sprite prompts can end up taking many re-generations and even sometimes prompt retweakings to get right. And that's all fine, but since it'll be the human doing all that iterating and judging, they should be given as much help as possible in terms of making sure they're assessing the correctness of all the details correctly. As described above, there are many situations where you should inform the human about decisions about details, especially any character details intentionally left out and any details added in that weren't explicit from the text. Do so with a python comment after the subarray like so:

    ["c-parker", crefs, cprompt, "Parker, the butler of Basil's estate. Make him middle aged with short medium-hatched hair with long sideburns."], # The sideburns are an added detail not from the text. Left out blue eye color.
