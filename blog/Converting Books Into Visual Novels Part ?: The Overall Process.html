Public Domain Pulp's first fourteen visuals novels — from Cup of Gold to Journey to the Center of the Earth — were all made with same general five (primary) steps:

  1. Have an LLM convert a book.txt file to a pulp.txt file, adding in line breaks, (n)ame identifiers, (s)peaker identifiers, and (e)xpression tags.
  2. Populate the remainder of the non-visual metdata required by pulp.txt by hand.
  3. Generate the sprite set (as determined by the prior metadata).
  4. Generate the background set (as determined by the prior metadata).
  5. Visually review and edit the final visual metadata.

And from these steps, my plan was to create a matching "Converting Books Into Visual Novels" blog post documenting the process for each step. Easy enough.

Or perhaps not so easy, since it turns out detailed technical writing is hard, and there were many additional substeps that warranted documentation (Part 0, Part 0.5, and Part 2±0.5), and also the subprocesses involved in each particular step kept evolving over time — each new visual novel has been a learning experience for engineering better results and better process efficiency. The end result being: as of right now, only the Part 1 step has been fully documented as a blog post, with Part 2 still being a draft. And both are, to some extent, essentially already obsolete!

LLMs' contextual intelligence has progressed wonderfully in the past year, such that what was impossible in January is now very much possible here in October. The whole reasoning behind separating Parts 1 and 2 of the conversion process was that LLMs were formerly capable of processing only a restricted subset of the metadata tags in initial book.txt-to-pulp.txt conversion step and that trying to get them to produce all the pre-visual metadata all at once would only waste time: it would be too inconsistent to be usable across a book-length text.

But not so any more. LLMs have gotten much more capable (and agentic), such that the new five step process is looking to turn into this:

  1. Have an LLM convert a book.txt file to a pulp.txt file, adding in line breaks and all types of tag metadata that can be set prior to the visual edit.
  2. Quickly review and tweak the outputted pulp.txt by hand.
  3. Generate the sprite set (as determined by the prior metadata).
  4. Generate the background set (as determined by the prior metadata).
  5. Visually review and edit the final visual metadata.

So now I'm mulling over these questions: Is it worth it to finish and publish the Part 2 draft? Do I go back and fully rewrite the Part 1 post to create Part 1 (v2)? In the time it takes me to rewrite Parts 1 and 2, will the whole process change again from further hands-on experience and further LLM intelligence progress? Should I just jump ahead and write up Parts 3, 4, and 5, which haven't yet quite so fundamentally changed, while waiting for the dust to settle on Parts 1 and 2?

I wish documentation was less time-consuming such that I could manifest all the disjointed ideas in my head into a well-written cohesive written structure, without loss of informational value. Then I wouldn't feel so bad about documentating and re-documentating and re-versioning repeated blog posts!

But until LLMs can extract thoughts for all their informational contents, I think I'm improvise. So what follows will be a very short, very high-level explanation of the current conversion process, with the current set of prompts being used, but without any guarantee of granular detail or reproducibility or consistency going forward.

1. The LLM Creates pulp.txt

The previous version of this step (documented as Converting Books Into Visual Novels Part 1: The First Edit — Creating the Starter pulp.txt) involved breaking a book into chapters, feeding each chapter individually into Gemini 3.1 Pro, having it create its limited subset of the metadata, then rebuilding the chapter parts all back into one file, fixing up the many compiler errors created from Gemini's various goofs.

The updated process is conceptually simpler: we can feed the entire book all at once to GPT-6 Astra via Codex, where it can invoke the Pulpifier compiler directly to iteratively build the entire pulp.txt file, where it can handle populating a much larger subset of the metadata, including those tags I had previously declared that an LLM shouldn't be involved with setting: (b)ackgrounds and (a)ages and e(x)tras and so on.

The full prompt given to Astra is pulp-prompt.txt, and it actually contains a lot of the same explanations, heuristics, and example that were going to go into the eventual Part 2 blog post. Except that, since it's written as an LLM prompt, it's rather less readable from a human perspective that would I'd ever publish under a "Blog" banner. (Not that my actual blog-writing abilities are that much better, but at least in this context, I follow the rules of paragraph breaks, if nothing else.)

2. The Human Edits pulp.txt

The previous version of this step also involved re-reading through the book via pulp.txt, except that now, instead of having to assign all the backgrounds and sprite modifiers and so forth, the human (me!) can just review what the LLM put down, making tweaks and metadata edits as-needed.

Perhaps in the far future of say, ten more months from now, a human-led review won't even be needed. Just have the LLMs — smarter LLMs, and more LLMs.

But for now, having a human in the loop still seems useful in practice, if nothing else than because the full set of heuristics the human (me!) has in his head (my head!) is still imperfectly communicated in pulp-prompt.txt, and so even a perfectly smart LLM might fail to produce perfect results if constrained by the stupid human's inability (my inability!) to fully formulate their thoughts into instructions.

3. Generate The Sprite Set

This has two substeps: first creating the image prompts, and then running the prompts against a prompt generator (and iterating). But even though this overall process hasn't fundamentally changed since the beginning of the site, the specifics of it have also changed from the recent LLM intelligence improvements.

Instead of going [human-written prompt -> AI-generated images], the process has become, roughly: [human-written vibes -> LLM-written prompts -> AI-generated images]. Which may sound like making the process unnecessarily longer and more convoluted for no reason, but this was a change motivated again by all the past experience around process efficiency and end-result quality, which adding this extra LLM-processing substep can actually help with.

It's hard to fully explain why and how without writing up the full Part 3 documentation blog post, so I'll just it leave it at again pasting in the image-prompting–prompting prompt c-prompts-prompt.txt that's fed to the LLM. Again, there's a lot of heuristics and imperfectly formulated explanation in there that can help give an idea of the overall goals of the step, but they're all again written for the LLM and not for the human.

4. Generate The Background Set

At a high level, there are basically the same considerations that go into this step as the previous. We want backgrounds, and we need the image generator to create them, and we need prompts to feed to the image generator, and it ultimately ends up easier and better to have the LLM write the prompts based off of human-written vibes than to human-write the prompts themselves.

The corresponding LLM instructions are in b-prompts-prompt.txt, with more corresponding heuristics and concepts-of-a-blog-post contained therein.

5. Visually Edit the Visual Novel

Of the five steps, this is the one that has changed least from the recent LLM intelligence improvements. Fundamentally, the human still needs to read through the VN, as a VN, and determine, as human, whether the VN looks good from a human perspective, making edits as-needed.

There's a lot more that goes into it than that, and so eventually this will need to be a blog post, but I fear it may take some time to get through all the preliminary explanations required from Parts 1-4.

The End (of the current process)

And that's it! Follow the above five poorly explained steps, and you too will be able to convert any book of your own into a visual novel in no time!
