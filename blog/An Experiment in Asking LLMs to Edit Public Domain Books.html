I created the Public Domain Diffs repository with two ideas in mind:

First, that LLMs are good at editing. Not perfect at editing, but neither are books perfect. So in theory, a sufficiently smart LLM could improve upon the published works of respected authors. (Where I get to decide what is and isn't an improvement, on account of my having good taste.)

Second, that the type of edits that current LLMs are best-suited for — local, targetted changes — are also exactly the type of changes that diff viewers are built for.

Together, the plan became to combine the smartness of LLMs with the tooling of version control: I'd create a repository that had full-text versions of famous books as its root master branch with LLM-edited child branches off of it:

Each LLM's edit of each book would be captured in an individual commit to its LLM branch, making the LLMs' edits easy (in theory) to examine:

Provided with ten famous(-ish) public domain texts: Could the LLMs find areas for prose improvements? Could they make those good edits while avoiding making bad or superfluous edits?

(For crappy source texts, we already know the answers to these questions are "yes" and "mostly yes". Hence why we want the more interesting challenge of running against more highly regarded works. The improvement potential should still be there, even if only more marginally so.)

This diff-based version controlled approach seemed the most efficient way to find out, with the added bonus of being able to compare the relative editing intelligence of competing LLMs.

The Setup

I knew from previous book-processing experiences that the LLMs were not yet good enough to process novel-length texts intelligently all in one go — the context size degradation would be too severe.

Instead, the plan would be to just break the books apart into chapter-length chunks, using a short looping script that fed the individual chapters into the LLM APIs with this prompt:

You are going to be given the task of editing a chapter from a famous public domain book, with the goal of improving its prose. Your edits can take any semantic form — adding, removing, restructuring, etc — using your own best judgment on what what prose changes have the potential to improve the existing text's quality. No author is perfect over the course of an entire book-length text, so I expect you to be able to identify some areas for enhancement.

At the same time, you should not over-edit — don't make lateral changes that lack a clear purpose. I expect you to be able to tell the difference between good vs mediocre prose and therefore when editing is unnecessary vs desirable. Also remember that the goal is to improve specifically the prose — not to change the overall book's identity. Don't butcher the text's plot, characters, or intended authorial voice — keep the scope of the editing targeted and local.

For this task, bear in mind that you'll only be provided with the text one chapter at-a-time. This shouldn't cause you any trouble in keeping track of the book's plot, which you already know, but it does mean that you won't be able to see how your parallel editing of the other chapters is going. To keep the scope of editing consistent across chapters then, you'll have to figure out your own internal editing-density Schelling point. (Left again to your own best judgment, since you know your own LLM brain better than I.)

The book you'll be editing is <book> by <author>.

Output the full text of the chapter back out to make it easy for me to diff the results against the original chapter to see what edits you've made. (And output juts the chapter text, starting with the chapter header.)

In the prompt, I tried to preempt several problems I foresaw that might hurt the editing:

  • I wanted to avoid under-editing.
  • I wanted to avoid over-editing.
  • I wanted to avoid editing scope/density inconsistencies between the independently processed chapters.
  • I wanted to avoid larger scale structural-type edits. (By necessity. Since even for those books that needed structural overhauls, it would be impossible with the independent chapter setup to keep such changes consistent.)
  • I wanted to avoid voice homogenization.

If the LLMs were at all competent, they would be able to avoid all of these pitfalls and also know to scale their editing inversely with the "tightness" of the various source texts.

That is, I had a specific least-editable-to-most-editable ordering in mind that I wanted to see borne out in the relative diff densities between the ten books:

  • The Sun Also Rises: Hemingway is a deeply annoying writer, but he's the best at being deeply annoying. Improving his prose without screwing up its characteristic style may prove impossible.
  • The Great Gatsby: Scott is just about as tight as Hemingway, so it's again difficult to think of what could even be improved upon. If the LLM can make any positive edits to either of these authors, it will be impressive.
  • Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hyde: Stevenson's prose is a little weaker prose-wise than the above two, but Jekyll and Hyde is still the most compact book of this set. (Partially, of course, because it's the shortest.) Perhaps a select few good edits can be mined from the text.
  • The Picture of Dorian Gray: Wilde's prose is excellent, but like Hemingway, his prose is also often annoying, and unlike Hemingway, the annoyance here is less justifiable. The LLM should have room here to cut back on some of Wilde's more obnoxious habits, though it will need to tread carefully about not screwing up the good stuff.
  • The Secret Garden: Burnett's prose is definitely less refined here than in any of the above books, but that's partially down to Secret Garden being a children's novel. Improving the text is definitely doable, but the improvements need to avoid screwing up the tone.
  • Pride and Prejudice: Austen's prose was great and groundbreaking for its time, but two centuries of writing technique refinements make the early weaknesses here more noticeable. That said, the book is still mostly good, even if it overstays its welcome. There's moderate room for improvement.
  • The Maltese Falcon: Of the three mystery pulp schlock novels here, Hammett's does the best at accomplishing via prose what it aims for. However, what it aims for still isn't all that great, so there should be plenty room for improvements, even while preserving the noir tone.
  • The Mysterious Affair at Styles: For a first novel, Christie does pretty good here, but the book still has at least one major problem and many more minor ones. Prose-wise, there should be plenty to tighten up.
  • A Study in Scarlet: This book is a real structural trashfire, and is the best example out of this set where the quality of the prose actively worsens over the course of the text. Conan Doyle's prose isn't particularly strong at any point in the text, but the LLM should still recognize that the second half needs more fixing than the first.
  • Cup of Gold: Both in concept and in execution, Cup of Gold is a bad book, and Steinbeck should feel bad for having written it. There should be no shortage of improvements the LLM can make to this text, even if the biggest improvement would probably be to just delete the entire thing.

The Problems

So with the loop, the prompt, and the predictions all set up, I went forward with running the experiment. And in examining the diffs… I ran into almost every problem I had tried to prevent, plus some additional ones.

The diffs were, in many places, a nighmare to read. The LLMs were fond of splitting and merging paragraphs — a fine type of edit to make in the abstract, except that it wrecked the ability to easily see what was actually being changed. I tried three different viewers, none of which was particularly readable:

Now, not all the diffs were borked like this, and interestingly, there were even differences between the different diff applications about which particular paragraphs were borked. So in theory with enough time, switching between different diff viewers and manually fixing the diff viewer line-ups, these paragraphs could have been properly examined.

But unfortunately, this problem combined with another problem of the diffs being way over-edited, almost universally throughout all the chapters of all the books. Not good edits either, but tons of meaningless word choice changes and comma adjustments and British-to-American (or the reversed) spelling adjustments and unicode character deletions that held no real impact on readability or prose quality.

And to make things worse, huge chunks of various points were inexplicably deleted at times, for no reason:

Bizarrely, reprompted with the same prompt for the same chapter, the output text would quit at about the same point each time, though not exactly the same point, so the results clearly weren't determinstic, yet they still managed to give up at about the same points consistently. (This in combination with the density of the diffs also being wildly off at times between adjacent chapters.)

So, with all these diffing issues, my original plan of carefully walking through all the diffs for each book through each chapter became wildly impractical.

Instead what I did was just skim the diffs, catalog some of the more interesting results, and then sit on them while I spent time trying to rethink how to better carry out the experiment to avoid the awful diff clutter.

But now a month-and-a-half has passed since actually doing and reading the diffs, and I now realize that, regardless of whether I have time to redo the experiment more correctly, I need to just go forward with presenting what this first pass found, even if the results are incompletely understood and somewhat unsatisfying to me:

Lateral Edits

As mentioned, the over-editing was extreme, with the vast majority of the results being nothing changes not even worth discussing. A more successful version of this experiment would have needed to find a better prompting approach that avoids all of the various meaningless edits to make the actually interesting ones easier to pull out.

That said, while most of the lateral edits were pointless, some more interesting categories did emerge:

Helping the Reader

One of the more curious behaviors of the LLMs was their tendency to try to throw a bone to the modern reader, seemingly to help them in places where older word usages might cause confusion, such as "flags" or "theatre" or "arcade" or "leaders" or "transit":
